## Abstract
Cont surveys and systematizes the empirical "stylized facts" of asset return distributions across asset classes and time periods. The key stylized facts documented are: (1) heavy tails (excess kurtosis, power-law tails); (2) absence of linear autocorrelation in returns; (3) long memory in absolute/squared returns (volatility clustering); (4) leverage effect (negative correlation between returns and future volatility); (5) aggregational normality (returns become more Gaussian at longer time scales); (6) non-Gaussianity even at moderate time scales; (7) intermittency (irregular burst of activity). This comprehensive inventory provides the empirical benchmarks any model of asset prices must reproduce.

## Key Contribution
Definitive catalog of empirical stylized facts for asset returns. Established the benchmark properties any good model must reproduce. Showed stylized facts are remarkably consistent across markets and time periods. Distinguished statistical issues (estimation, test power) from genuine economic effects. Widely used as a model validation checklist.

## Methods
Review and synthesis of empirical literature across equities, FX, commodities. Statistical tests: Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Hill estimator for tail index, Hurst exponent, autocorrelation functions of returns, |returns|, returns². Aggregational normality analysis across time scales. Leverage effect measurement via cross-correlation function.

## Results
Tail index α ≈ 3-5 for daily equity returns (finite variance, infinite kurtosis for some). Autocorrelation of returns ≈ 0 for lags > 20 minutes. Autocorrelation of |returns| decays slowly (~power law with exponent β ≈ 0.3). Leverage effect: Corr(r_t, |r_{t+k}|²) < 0 for k > 0. Aggregational normality: kurtosis decreases with time scale.

## Critique
Stylized facts are averages—individual markets and time periods show significant variation. Power-law tail estimation is sensitive to sample size and threshold. Long-memory vs. regime-switching debate: apparent long memory may be generated by mixing of different regimes. The "universality" of stylized facts across markets should not be overstated.

## Relevance
Essential reading before modeling any asset return distribution or volatility process. Every model (GARCH, stochastic volatility, jump-diffusion, rough volatility) should be evaluated against these stylized facts. This paper is the universal reference for "does my model look like real data?" in quantitative finance.

## Abstract
Samuelson provides a rigorous proof that in a competitive market where all information is immediately incorporated into prices, price changes must be unforecastable—i.e., prices follow a martingale. If prices consistently rose or fell predictably, rational traders would exploit this until the pattern disappeared. The proof requires only that future prices are correctly anticipated in expectation, not that markets are populated by rational agents. This established the mathematical foundations for the Efficient Market Hypothesis and demonstrated that Brownian motion (Geometric Brownian Motion for positive prices) is the natural model for asset prices.

## Key Contribution
Mathematical proof that competitive markets imply martingale prices. Rediscovered and publicized Bachelier's work. Provided the theoretical basis for GBM as a price model. Laid groundwork for the EMH (Fama 1970). Distinction between price levels (not martingales) and properly deflated/discounted prices (martingales).

## Methods
Futures/forward price theory. Proof by contradiction: predictable prices → arbitrage → contradiction. Conditional expectations and martingale property. Connection to Bachelier's Brownian motion model.

## Results
In equilibrium, price changes have zero conditional expected value (martingale property). Prices fluctuate randomly around their rational expected values. GBM provides the natural continuous-time model for positive asset prices.

## Critique
Strictly proves prices are fair games, not that markets are strong-form efficient. Does not rule out risk premia (expected returns can be non-zero). The "random walk" is a special case; the martingale is the general result.

## Relevance
The theoretical backbone of the EMH. Essential for understanding why alpha is hard to find and why simple price-forecasting rules fail. Directly inspired the Black-Scholes model's use of GBM and the risk-neutral pricing framework.

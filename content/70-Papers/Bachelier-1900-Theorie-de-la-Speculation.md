## Abstract
Bachelier's doctoral thesis introduced Brownian motion as a model for stock price movements on the Paris Bourse, predating Einstein's work on Brownian motion by five years. The thesis derives option pricing formulas using the heat equation, establishes that the mathematical expectation of a speculator is zero, and introduces the concept of a continuous-time random process for prices. Bachelier shows that price changes are independent and normally distributed, laying the foundation for the Efficient Market Hypothesis and all subsequent mathematical finance.

## Key Contribution
First mathematical model of financial markets. Introduced Brownian motion to model price dynamics. Derived the first option pricing formula. Established the "fair game" nature of speculation (zero expected profit). Founded quantitative finance as a discipline.

## Methods
Heat equation / diffusion equation. Normal distribution for price increments. Continuous-time stochastic process (pre-Itô). Derived option prices as integrals over the normal distribution. Applied to options ("primes") on the Paris Bourse.

## Results
Price changes follow a normal (Gaussian) distribution. The expected gain from speculation is zero—markets are informationally efficient. Derived closed-form prices for simple options. The variance of price changes grows linearly with time.

## Critique
Assumes prices follow arithmetic Brownian motion (can go negative). Gaussian returns understate tail risk. Market frictions and transaction costs ignored. Only recognized decades later (Samuelson rediscovered it in 1955; Itô's rigorous framework came later).

## Relevance
The "big bang" of quantitative finance. Every subsequent model—Black-Scholes, CAPM, EMH—builds on Bachelier's insight that prices follow stochastic processes. Essential historical context for understanding modern mathematical finance.

## Abstract
This landmark paper introduces the mean-variance framework for portfolio selection. Markowitz formalizes the idea that investors care about both expected return and variance of return, and shows that rational investors should hold portfolios on the efficient frontier—the set offering maximum expected return for a given level of variance. The key insight is that diversification reduces portfolio variance without necessarily reducing expected return, as long as asset returns are not perfectly correlated. The paper constructs the efficient frontier and poses portfolio selection as a quadratic programming problem.

## Key Contribution
Founded modern portfolio theory (MPT). Introduced the efficient frontier. Formalized diversification mathematically: portfolio variance depends on covariances, not just individual variances. Posed portfolio construction as mean-variance optimization. Earned Markowitz the 1990 Nobel Prize in Economics.

## Methods
Mean-variance optimization (quadratic programming). Efficient frontier construction. Covariance matrix of returns. Geometric interpretation in mean-variance space. Analysis of the role of correlation in portfolio risk.

## Results
The efficient frontier of optimal portfolios. All rational mean-variance investors hold frontier portfolios. Diversification is beneficial when asset correlations are less than 1. The minimum-variance portfolio is the leftmost point on the frontier.

## Critique
Requires estimation of a large covariance matrix (curse of dimensionality). Sensitive to input estimation errors ("garbage in, garbage out"). Quadratic utility function or normal returns required for strict validity. Ignores higher moments (skewness, kurtosis). Static single-period framework.

## Relevance
The foundation of all quantitative portfolio management. Required reading before any discussion of portfolio construction, factor models, or risk budgeting. CAPM, APT, Black-Litterman, and risk parity all extend this framework.

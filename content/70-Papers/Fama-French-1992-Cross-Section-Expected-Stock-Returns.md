## Abstract
Fama and French conduct a comprehensive cross-sectional study of US stock returns from 1963-1990 and find that beta has little explanatory power for expected returns once size (market capitalization) and value (book-to-market ratio) are included. Size and book-to-market are strongly and positively related to future returns, while beta is not. This paper effectively challenged the empirical validity of CAPM as a complete description of expected returns and directly motivated the Fama-French three-factor model (1993). It is the empirical companion to FF (1993) and together they represent the most influential work in empirical asset pricing.

## Key Contribution
Documented the failure of CAPM in cross-sectional data. Showed that beta is "dead" as the sole predictor of expected returns. Identified size and B/M as the two most powerful predictors of cross-sectional returns. Directly motivated the FF three-factor model (1993). The most influential empirical paper in asset pricing.

## Methods
NYSE, AMEX, NASDAQ stocks 1963-1990. Cross-sectional (Fama-MacBeth) regressions of monthly returns on beta, size (log ME), book-to-market (log BE/ME), E/P ratio, leverage. Sorting into decile portfolios on various characteristics.

## Results
Beta alone: not significant in cross-section when controlling for size. Size: strongly negative coefficient (small stocks earn more). B/M: strongly positive coefficient (high B/M = value stocks earn more). Size and B/M together: R² ≈ 6% in monthly cross-sections (high for cross-sectional regressions). E/P and leverage absorbed by size + B/M.

## Critique
Data mining concerns: B/M chosen retrospectively. The value premium has weakened since publication. Interpretation debate: risk factor (FF) vs. mispricing (Lakonishok et al.). Basu (1977) documented value premium earlier using E/P ratio. Replication studies confirm results on average but with significant time variation.

## Relevance
This paper established the empirical foundation for the Fama-French three-factor model. It is required reading for anyone working in factor investing, empirical asset pricing, or quantitative equity. The B/M ratio (or variations like Price-to-Book) became the canonical value factor signal.

## Abstract
Brunnermeier and Pedersen develop a theory linking market liquidity (the ease of trading assets at low cost) and funding liquidity (the ease of obtaining financing for asset positions). Traders who face funding constraints reduce positions when prices fall, amplifying price drops and further reducing market liquidity—creating liquidity spirals. The model shows that: (1) market liquidity can suddenly evaporate (multiple equilibria with low/high liquidity); (2) liquidity commonality arises naturally (liquidity dries up across all assets simultaneously when funding is tight); (3) liquidity spirals are self-reinforcing. The theory explains the 2007-2009 financial crisis mechanism.

## Key Contribution
Unified market liquidity and funding liquidity in a single model. Derived liquidity spirals: falling prices → margin calls → fire sales → further price falls. Explained why liquidity correlates across assets during crises. Showed flight to quality as a rational response to funding constraints. Transformed understanding of systemic financial risk.

## Methods
Model of speculative traders (arbitrageurs) with funding constraints. Margin constraints: speculators' positions limited by capital/margin. Multiple equilibria: high-liquidity and low-liquidity equilibria coexist. Comparative statics on margin requirements and market liquidity. Value at Risk (VaR) margin as endogenous feedback mechanism.

## Results
Market liquidity and funding liquidity are mutually reinforcing. Liquidity spirals: small shocks → tight margins → forced selling → wider spreads → tighter margins. Multiple equilibria: liquid and illiquid market states. Systemically important: arbitrageurs' capital level determines which equilibrium prevails. Predicts commonality in liquidity across seemingly unrelated assets.

## Critique
Stylized model—no explicit price dynamics or LOB. Rational expectations assumed (no behavioral amplification). Empirical testing requires identifying funding liquidity separately. Counter-cyclical capital requirements partially address but don't eliminate spirals. Post-crisis regulatory capital requirements (Basel III) motivated by this model.

## Relevance
Fundamental for understanding financial crises, systemic risk, and the role of leverage in markets. The "liquidity spiral" concept directly influenced Basel III (liquidity coverage ratio, NSFR) and stress testing. Every risk manager must understand how market and funding liquidity interact during stress. Essential for hedge fund risk management, prime brokerage, and regulatory policy.

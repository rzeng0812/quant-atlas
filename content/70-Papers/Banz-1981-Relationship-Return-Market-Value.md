## Abstract
Banz documents the first major empirical anomaly to CAPM: small market-capitalization stocks earn systematically higher risk-adjusted returns than large-cap stocks on the NYSE from 1936-1975. After controlling for CAPM beta, small stocks outperform by approximately 0.4% per month. This "size effect" cannot be attributed to higher systematic risk, suggesting either market inefficiency or a missing risk factor. The finding directly motivated multi-factor models—the Fama-French SMB (small minus big) factor is a direct formalization of the size premium.

## Key Contribution
First documentation of the size effect (small-cap premium). Major evidence against CAPM's completeness. Motivated the SMB factor in Fama-French (1993). Sparked 40+ years of research on cross-sectional return anomalies. Established market capitalization as an important variable in asset pricing.

## Methods
NYSE stocks 1936-1975. Five size quintile portfolios. Cross-sectional regression of returns on beta and log(market cap). Time-series analysis of quintile returns. Risk-adjustment using CAPM alpha.

## Results
Small stocks earn 0.4%/month more than large stocks after CAPM adjustment. Effect is non-linear: concentrated in the smallest quintile. Effect is persistent over multiple decades. Small stocks have higher betas, but the extra return exceeds the CAPM risk premium.

## Critique
Effect has weakened since documentation (publication effect). Concentrated in January (January effect). May be partially explained by illiquidity premium. Small-cap stocks have higher transaction costs, eroding the premium for most investors. Data mining concerns.

## Relevance
Foundational evidence for factor investing beyond CAPM. The size factor (SMB) in Fama-French is directly motivated by Banz. Any factor model discussion should reference the size effect as the first documented anomaly. Still actively debated: Is it a risk factor? A liquidity premium? Or a mispricing?

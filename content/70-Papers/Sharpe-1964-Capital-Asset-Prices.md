## Abstract
Sharpe derives the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) from Markowitz's portfolio theory under the assumption that all investors hold mean-variance efficient portfolios and markets clear. In equilibrium, every investor holds a combination of the risk-free asset and the market portfolio. The expected return of any asset equals the risk-free rate plus a risk premium proportional to beta (the asset's covariance with the market divided by market variance). Systematic (market) risk commands a premium; idiosyncratic risk can be diversified away and earns nothing.

## Key Contribution
Derived CAPM—the first asset pricing model with a closed-form equilibrium. Introduced beta as the measure of systematic risk. Showed that only non-diversifiable risk is priced. Provided the Security Market Line (SML). Earned Sharpe the 1990 Nobel Prize in Economics.

## Methods
General equilibrium framework. Markowitz mean-variance optimization as investor behavior. Market clearing conditions. Derivation of the security market line: E[Ri] = Rf + βi(E[Rm] - Rf).

## Results
Every investor holds the same risky portfolio (the market portfolio). The Capital Market Line (CML) describes efficient portfolios. The Security Market Line (SML) prices all assets. Beta is the only relevant risk measure in equilibrium.

## Critique
Unrealistic assumptions: homogeneous expectations, no taxes/transaction costs, unlimited borrowing at risk-free rate. Beta estimated with error. Empirically, beta alone explains little of cross-sectional return variation (Fama-French 1992). Roll's critique: the true market portfolio is unobservable.

## Relevance
The starting point for all asset pricing theory. CAPM is the null hypothesis against which all factor models are tested. Beta remains universally used in cost of capital calculations, performance attribution (Jensen's alpha), and risk reporting.

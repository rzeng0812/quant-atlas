> [!info] Problem Chain
> **Chain:** ML-Finance → Gap: The majority of financial information relevant to price formation (earnings calls, filings, news) is unstructured text that classical factor models can't directly ingest
> **This concept:** Uses LLM text embeddings and LLM-agent reasoning to turn earnings calls, 10-K/10-Q disclosures, and news into cross-sectional alpha factors — while treating the resulting signal's genuine predictive power as an open, actively contested question rather than a given
> **Alternative approaches to this gap:** classical NLP sentiment scoring — bag-of-words and dictionary methods such as Loughran-McDonald, the pre-LLM baseline
> **You need first:** [[Alpha Factor]], [[Feature Engineering Finance]]
> **This unlocks:** [[Overfitting and Multiple Testing]]

## Why This Exists

**The gap:** Roughly half of the information that moves prices around earnings — management's tone on a call, hedged language in a 10-K risk section, a supplier's disclosure about demand — arrives as prose, not numbers. Classical factor construction (see [[Alpha Factor]]) is built for numeric time series: prices, volumes, fundamentals ratios. It has no native way to consume "we are cautiously optimistic about second-half demand, though we are seeing some softness in certain channels."

**What came before:** Dictionary-based sentiment scoring — count words from a curated finance-specific wordlist (the Loughran-McDonald dictionary is the canonical example: separate lists of "positive," "negative," "uncertainty," and "litigious" words tuned for 10-K language, since general-English dictionaries mislabel terms like "liability" or "tax" as negative). This is fast, transparent, and fully auditable, but it is a bag-of-words method: it has no model of context, negation, or sarcasm, and it cannot represent *relationships between firms*. "Revenue declined less than expected" and "revenue declined more than expected" can score identically under naive word-counting.

**What this adds:** Three genuinely new capabilities that dictionary methods structurally cannot provide:
1. **Semantic embeddings.** A transformer encoder (e.g., FinBERT) maps a document to a dense vector that captures contextual meaning — negation, hedging, comparative language — rather than word counts. The vault's own evidence for this: in *Which Voices Move Markets*, FinBERT-derived, speaker-weighted sentiment **entirely subsumes** the Loughran-McDonald dictionary score in a combined regression (FinBERT t = 5.90 vs. LM t = 0.86).
2. **Network/graph propagation.** Because embeddings live in a shared vector space, firms can be linked by textual similarity, supply-chain relationships, or LLM-classified economic relations, and a signal can propagate along that graph — something no dictionary method offers. This is the core idea in both *Supply Chain Propagation of Textual Signals* (propagation through a supply-chain knowledge graph) and *Cross-Stock Predictability via LLM-Augmented Semantic Networks* (an LLM filters a candidate embedding-similarity graph down to edges with genuine economic content before propagating).
3. **Agentic, multi-step reasoning.** Rather than a single sentiment score, a system of specialist LLM agents (news, fundamentals, price dynamics, macro) can synthesize a monthly investment thesis — the architecture used in *Signal or Noise in Multi-Agent LLM-based Stock Recommendations?* (MarketSenseAI).

**What it still doesn't solve:** This is the part the hype cycle tends to skip. Two problems are specific to LLM-based signals and have no clean analogue in classical factor research:
- **Training-data contamination / look-ahead bias.** An LLM is trained on a large internet corpus up to some cutoff date. If you use that model to embed a 10-K filed in, say, 2016, and then "backtest" whether the embedding predicted 2016–2017 returns, you cannot fully rule out that the model's pretraining corpus already contains retrospective commentary, news coverage, or analysis of what actually happened to that company afterward. Unlike a momentum factor computed from raw prices, an LLM factor is generated by a model whose weights encode *some* knowledge of the future relative to the text's nominal timestamp. This is a different and arguably harder problem than the look-ahead bias already discussed in [[Alpha Factor]] and [[Overfitting and Multiple Testing]].
- **Researcher degrees of freedom multiply.** Choice of embedding model, prompt wording, agent architecture, graph-construction threshold, and propagation weighting are all new knobs a researcher can (consciously or not) tune until a backtest looks good — compounding the multiple-testing problem that already afflicts classical factor discovery.
- **The question is genuinely unsettled.** The vault's own *Signal or Noise* paper poses the question in its title for a reason: even a carefully validated multi-agent system finds a statistically significant edge on one universe (S&P 500) but only a directionally consistent, not-significant result on another (S&P 100) — a reminder that "the system looked good in one test" is not the same as "the system has genuine, robust skill."

## Math Concepts

### From embedding to factor score

An encoder (FinBERT, or a general-purpose LLM embedding endpoint) maps a document $d_{i,t}$ for firm $i$ at time $t$ to a dense vector:

$$\mathbf{e}_{i,t} = \text{Encoder}(d_{i,t}) \in \mathbb{R}^{k}$$

where $k$ is the embedding dimension (e.g., 768 for FinBERT-base). This vector alone is not a factor — [[Alpha Factor|alpha factors]] are scalars per stock per date. The standard approach (used in *Supply Chain Propagation of Textual Signals*) is to find the direction in embedding space that historically predicted returns via a **Fama-MacBeth cross-sectional regression**. At each date $t$, regress next-period returns on the embedding, plus controls:

$$r_{i,t+1} = \alpha_t + \boldsymbol{\beta}_t^\top \mathbf{e}_{i,t} + \gamma_t^\top \mathbf{X}_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}$$

where $\mathbf{X}_{i,t}$ are controls (momentum, volatility, size). Running this regression date-by-date produces a time series of coefficient vectors $\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_t\}$; the average $\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \boldsymbol{\beta}_t$ defines the return-predictive direction. The scalar factor score is then the projection of the embedding onto that direction:

$$f_{i,t} = \bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^\top \mathbf{e}_{i,t}$$

This is exactly the same cross-sectional-regression logic as any other Fama-MacBeth factor test — the only difference is that the "characteristic" being regressed is a high-dimensional learned embedding rather than a hand-built ratio.

### Network-augmented propagation

A firm's own disclosure is not the only text that's informative about it — its suppliers, customers, and textually-similar peers carry relevant signal too. Let $W \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ be a firm-firm weight matrix, where $W_{ij} > 0$ if firm $j$ is linked to firm $i$ (supply-chain relationship, or an edge that an LLM has classified as economically meaningful, as in *Cross-Stock Predictability via LLM-Augmented Semantic Networks*, where LLM-based edge filtering is what distinguishes a genuine economic link from spurious textual proximity). The **network-augmented embedding** propagates neighbor information into the firm's own signal, one graph "hop" at a time:

$$\mathbf{e}_{i,t}^{\text{net}} = \mathbf{e}_{i,t} + \gamma \sum_{j} \tilde{W}_{ij}\, \mathbf{e}_{j,t}, \qquad \tilde{W}_{ij} = \frac{W_{ij}}{\sum_j W_{ij}}$$

with $\gamma$ a propagation strength and $\tilde{W}$ row-normalized (equivalent to one layer of a graph-convolution-style neighbor-averaging operation). In matrix form for the full cross-section: $\mathbf{E}_t^{\text{net}} = \mathbf{E}_t + \gamma \tilde{W} \mathbf{E}_t = (I + \gamma \tilde{W})\mathbf{E}_t$. Relation-aware weighting (as in the Cross-Stock paper) further scales $\tilde W_{ij}$ by the LLM's classified relation type and by distance in the graph, so a direct customer-supplier edge counts more than a two-hop indirect link.

### Newey-West-adjusted significance testing

Fama-MacBeth coefficients $\{\hat\beta_t\}_{t=1}^T$ form a time series, and the naive standard error $\hat\sigma_{\hat\beta}/\sqrt{T}$ assumes they are i.i.d. — false, because overlapping holding periods and persistent disclosure content induce autocorrelation. The **Newey-West** estimator corrects the standard error for both heteroskedasticity and serial correlation up to lag $L$:

$$\widehat{\text{Var}}_{NW}(\bar\beta) = \frac{1}{T^2}\left[\sum_{t=1}^T \hat u_t^2 + 2\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(1 - \frac{\ell}{L+1}\right)\sum_{t=\ell+1}^{T} \hat u_t \hat u_{t-\ell}\right], \qquad \hat u_t = \hat\beta_t - \bar\beta$$

with the Bartlett kernel weight $\left(1 - \frac{\ell}{L+1}\right)$ downweighting higher lags. The reported significance statistic is:

$$t_{NW} = \frac{\bar\beta}{\sqrt{\widehat{\text{Var}}_{NW}(\bar\beta)}}$$

This is the exact statistic *Supply Chain Propagation of Textual Signals* reports for its network-augmented factor ($t_{NW} = -2.64$) — using the plain, non-adjusted standard error on the same data would very likely overstate significance, since MD&A language and supply-chain structure are both highly persistent from quarter to quarter.

## Walkthrough

Following *Supply Chain Propagation of Textual Signals* end to end, as a concrete worked example:

**Step 1 — Embed the disclosures.** For 255 S&P 500 firms, 2011–2025, extract the MD&A (Management's Discussion and Analysis) section of each annual 10-K and encode it with FinBERT to get $\mathbf{e}_{i,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$ per firm-year.

**Step 2 — Build two versions of the signal.** (a) *Direct embeddings*: use $\mathbf{e}_{i,t}$ as-is. (b) *Network-augmented embeddings*: propagate through a supply-chain knowledge graph, $\mathbf{e}_{i,t}^{\text{net}} = \mathbf{e}_{i,t} + \gamma \sum_j \tilde W_{ij}\mathbf{e}_{j,t}$, so a firm's factor score also reflects what its suppliers and customers are disclosing.

**Step 3 — Cross-sectional regression.** Each period, run Fama-MacBeth: forward return on the embedding-derived factor, controlling for momentum, volatility, and size. Average the coefficient across periods and Newey-West adjust.

**Step 4 — Result.** The network-augmented factor is statistically significant ($t_{NW} = -2.64$) even after the controls — the *direct*, non-propagated embedding factor is comparatively weaker, consistent with the paper's headline claim that network structure carries pricing-relevant information beyond what a firm says about itself.

**Step 5 — Portfolio construction.** Sort stocks into deciles on the network-augmented factor score each period; go long the top decile, short the bottom decile, rebalance. Reported performance: **annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.86**, **Fama-French five-factor alpha of 7.27% per year**. The predictive power is reported to survive out-of-sample testing, placebo experiments (randomizing the graph structure), sector-neutralization, and subsample analysis — the standard toolkit for defending against the objection that the result is a data-mining artifact (see Analysis below).

Sharpe 0.86 is a solid but not extraordinary long-short result — well within the range of a "real, tradeable, but not spectacular" factor, similar in magnitude to many classical equity factors before costs.

## Analysis

This is the section where hype and evidence need to be pulled apart carefully. The vault's own literature does not agree on a single verdict, and that disagreement is the honest state of the field.

**The skeptical baseline — why "Signal or Noise" is the right title for this literature.** *Signal or Noise in Multi-Agent LLM-based Stock Recommendations?* is explicitly designed to interrogate, not assume, that an LLM-agent system adds value. Its most important methodological choice is generating trading signals **live**, at each observation date, specifically to **eliminate look-ahead bias** — the system cannot have "seen the future" because the signal is produced in real time with no retrospective access to outcomes. This is a direct, structural answer to the training-data-contamination problem described above. The result on the S&P 500 cohort is a genuinely significant edge (+2.18%/month strong-buy vs. +1.15%/month passive benchmark, 99.7th percentile of 10,000 Monte Carlo random-selection portfolios, $p = 0.003$; cross-sectional IC significant at ICIR = +0.489, $p = 0.024$). But the S&P 100 cohort tells a more cautionary story: a directionally consistent +30.5% compound excess return that **does not reach statistical significance**, limited by an average selection of only ~10 stocks per month — an illustration of Grinold's Fundamental Law (see [[Alpha Factor]]): low breadth caps the achievable information ratio even when the underlying signal quality (IC) is real. A system can look like it's working and still fail a rigorous significance bar on a narrower universe. That asymmetry — real signal on one test, unresolved on another — is exactly what "signal or noise" as an open question looks like in practice, and it argues against treating any single positive backtest as proof.

**The look-ahead-bias / data-contamination risk is specific to LLMs.** Most of the literature in this space — including *Cross-Stock Predictability via LLM-Augmented Semantic Networks* and *Supply Chain Propagation of Textual Signals* — evaluates historical text (10-Ks filed years in the past) with a pretrained LLM whose training cutoff postdates the filing date, sometimes by years. Unlike a price-based momentum factor, where the raw input data genuinely could not have "known" the future, an LLM's weights are a compressed function of a training corpus that may already contain post-hoc analysis, news retrospectives, or even the eventual stock-price outcome tied to that filing. This is not a hypothetical concern; it is the single biggest reason to treat historical-text LLM backtests with more skepticism than a comparable price-based factor backtest, even when the reported statistics look clean.

**How credible papers defend against this.** None of these defenses fully close the contamination gap, but each addresses a real, distinct failure mode:
- **Live signal generation** (*Signal or Noise*) — the strongest defense, because it removes the possibility of retrospective leakage entirely, at the cost of only being usable going forward, not for historical validation.
- **Explicit temporal out-of-sample splits** (*Which Voices Move Markets*) — the IC actually *improves* out-of-sample (0.142) relative to in-sample (0.115), which is the opposite of what naive overfitting would predict, and is a genuinely reassuring result, though it does not by itself rule out corpus contamination for older transcripts.
- **Placebo tests, sector-neutralization, and subsample analysis** (*Supply Chain Propagation of Textual Signals*) — designed to catch spurious graph structure or sector-driven confounds, not training-data leakage per se.
- **Monte Carlo permutation benchmarking** (*Signal or Noise*) — comparing the observed result against a distribution of random-selection portfolios (10,000 draws) is a strong test that the result isn't attributable to generic market drift or luck, though it doesn't test for pretraining contamination either.
- **Controlling for known factors** (momentum, volatility, size, SUE across multiple papers) — necessary to confirm the LLM signal isn't simply re-deriving a classical factor under a new name.
- **Explainability checks** (*Shapley in Context*) — Shapley-value attribution shows which tokens/phrases drive a financial-language model's output, and whether those attributions are consistent with established financial reasoning. This doesn't validate predictive power directly, but a signal whose attributions make no financial sense is a red flag for a spurious correlation rather than genuine information extraction.

**Bottom line.** The evidence in this vault's own papers is more measured than the "LLMs read earnings calls and print alpha" narrative suggests: there is real, statistically defensible signal in some settings (speaker-weighted FinBERT sentiment subsuming dictionary methods; live multi-agent recommendations beating passive and random baselines on a broad universe), genuine methodological rigor being applied by the better papers, and simultaneously an unresolved, LLM-specific look-ahead-bias risk that has no equivalent in classical factor research and that no single paper in this set fully closes for backtests on historical text.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

# --- Step 1: embed a batch of documents (pseudocode for the encoder call) ---

def embed_documents(documents: dict[str, str], model="finbert") -> pd.DataFrame:

    Embed a batch of firm disclosures (e.g., 10-K MD&A sections) into
    dense vectors.

        embeddings[ticker] = _encoder_call(text, model=model)
    return pd.DataFrame(embeddings).T  # tickers x dims

# --- Step 2: network-augmented propagation (optional) ---

def propagate_embeddings(embeddings: pd.DataFrame, weight_matrix: pd.DataFrame,
                          gamma: float = 0.3) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """
    Propagate embeddings across a firm-firm relationship graph
    (e.g., supply-chain links, or LLM-filtered co-mention edges).

    embeddings    : DataFrame (tickers x dims)
    weight_matrix : DataFrame (tickers x tickers), row-normalized weights
    """
    W = weight_matrix.reindex(index=embeddings.index, columns=embeddings.index).fillna(0.0)
    row_sums = W.sum(axis=1).replace(0, np.nan)
    W_norm = W.div(row_sums, axis=0).fillna(0.0)
    return embeddings + gamma * W_norm.values @ embeddings


# --- Step 3: cross-sectional Fama-MacBeth regression, period by period ---

def fama_macbeth_embedding_factor(embeddings_by_date: dict[pd.Timestamp, pd.DataFrame],
                                   forward_returns: pd.DataFrame,
                                   controls_by_date: dict[pd.Timestamp, pd.DataFrame] = None
                                   ) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """
    For each date, regress forward returns on the embedding (plus optional
    controls). Returns the time series of embedding coefficient vectors.
    """
    betas = {}
    for date, emb in embeddings_by_date.items():
        common = emb.index.intersection(forward_returns.columns)
        if len(common) < 20:
            continue
        y = forward_returns.loc[date, common].values
        X = emb.loc[common].values
        if controls_by_date is not None and date in controls_by_date:
            X = np.hstack([X, controls_by_date[date].loc[common].values])
        X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(common)), X])  # intercept
        beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
        betas[date] = beta[1:1 + emb.shape[1]]  # drop intercept, keep embedding coefs
    return pd.DataFrame(betas).T  # dates x embedding dims


def newey_west_tstat(beta_series: np.ndarray, lags: int = 4) -> float:
    """
    Newey-West adjusted t-statistic for the mean of a Fama-MacBeth
    coefficient time series (Bartlett kernel, given lag truncation).
    """
    T = len(beta_series)
    beta_bar = beta_series.mean()
    u = beta_series - beta_bar

    gamma0 = np.sum(u ** 2) / T
    var = gamma0
    for lag in range(1, lags + 1):
        w = 1 - lag / (lags + 1)
        gamma_lag = np.sum(u[lag:] * u[:-lag]) / T
        var += 2 * w * gamma_lag

    se = np.sqrt(var / T)
    return beta_bar / se


# --- Putting it together: project onto the average predictive direction ---

def build_factor_score(embeddings: pd.DataFrame, beta_bar: np.ndarray) -> pd.Series:
    """Project each firm's embedding onto the estimated predictive direction."""
    return embeddings.values @ beta_bar


# Usage sketch (synthetic data, illustrative only):
np.random.seed(0)
dates = pd.date_range("2020-01-01", periods=24, freq="M")
tickers = [f"STK{i:03d}" for i in range(50)]

embeddings_by_date = {
    d: pd.DataFrame(np.random.randn(50, 16), index=tickers) for d in dates
}
forward_returns = pd.DataFrame(np.random.randn(len(dates), 50) * 0.05,
                                index=dates, columns=tickers)

beta_series = fama_macbeth_embedding_factor(embeddings_by_date, forward_returns)
beta_bar = beta_series.mean().values
t_stats = [newey_west_tstat(beta_series[col].values) for col in beta_series.columns]

print(f"Mean |NW t-stat| across embedding dims: {np.mean(np.abs(t_stats)):.2f}")
```

The synthetic example above will not produce a meaningful t-stat — it's random noise by construction. The point is the pipeline shape: embed → (optionally propagate through a graph) → cross-sectional regress → Newey-West test → project into a scalar factor usable exactly like any other input to [[Alpha Factor|alpha factor]] construction (winsorize, z-score, sector-neutralize, evaluate IC).

## Bridge to Quant / ML

- An LLM-derived factor is still, in the end, just a factor: once you have $f_{i,t}$, it goes through the exact same [[Alpha Factor]] pipeline — winsorization, cross-sectional z-scoring, sector- and market-neutralization, IC evaluation — as a momentum or value factor. LLMs change *how the raw signal is constructed*, not the discipline required to validate it.
- [[Overfitting and Multiple Testing]] applies with extra force here: an LLM-alpha research process has strictly more researcher degrees of freedom than a classical factor search (choice of encoder, prompt, agent architecture, graph threshold, propagation weighting), which inflates the effective number of hypotheses tested and should push the required significance bar higher, not lower, than the 3-sigma standard already recommended for classical factor discovery.
- Deploying any LLM-derived signal in a regulated investment process raises an explainability requirement that dictionary methods largely sidestepped by being transparent-by-construction. *Shapley in Context* is directly relevant: Shapley-value attribution over financial text gives a way to check whether an LLM's output is driven by financially sensible language rather than a spurious correlation, which matters both for scientific credibility and for satisfying compliance/regulatory review of a black-box signal.

---

### Level 1 — Conceptual

**Q1.** Why does the vault's evidence suggest that LLM embeddings are a meaningfully different tool than dictionary-based sentiment scoring, rather than just a fancier version of the same thing?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Dictionary methods (like Loughran-McDonald) count occurrences of pre-classified words with no model of context, negation, or comparison between firms. LLM embeddings capture contextual meaning (in <em>Which Voices Move Markets</em>, FinBERT-based sentiment entirely subsumes the LM dictionary score in a joint regression — FinBERT t = 5.90 vs. LM t = 0.86) and, because embeddings live in a continuous vector space, they support entirely new operations dictionary methods cannot do at all, such as propagating a signal across a supply-chain or co-mention graph (as in <em>Supply Chain Propagation of Textual Signals</em> and <em>Cross-Stock Predictability via LLM-Augmented Semantic Networks</em>). The difference is not just "better sentiment scoring" — it's a structurally different set of capabilities.
</details>

**Q2.** What is the look-ahead-bias risk that is specific to LLM-derived signals and has no direct equivalent in classical, price-based alpha factors?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
An LLM's weights are trained on a large corpus up to some cutoff date. If that model is then used to embed historical text (e.g., a 10-K filed years earlier) for a backtest, its pretraining corpus may already contain retrospective news coverage, analysis, or even the eventual outcome tied to that disclosure — meaning the "prediction" is not necessarily made from information available at the time. A momentum or value factor computed from raw historical prices has no equivalent risk, because the numeric inputs genuinely could not encode future information. This is the central reason to treat historical-text LLM backtests with more caution than comparable price-based factor backtests.
</details>

**Q3.** Why does the <em>Signal or Noise</em> paper generate its trading signals live, at each observation date, rather than running a standard retrospective backtest over historical transcripts?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Generating signals live means the model produces its recommendation in real time with no access to what actually happened afterward — this structurally eliminates the training-data-contamination risk described above, since there is no future information for the model to have implicitly absorbed about that specific observation. It is a direct methodological response to the look-ahead-bias critique, at the cost of only validating going forward (it can't be used to "backtest" further into the past without reintroducing the same contamination risk).
</details>

### Level 2 — Quantitative

**Q4.** <em>Supply Chain Propagation of Textual Signals</em> reports a Newey-West t-statistic of −2.64 for its network-augmented factor. Is this statistically significant at the 5% level, and what does the Newey-West adjustment protect against that a naive standard error would not?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
|t| = 2.64 exceeds the conventional two-sided 5% critical value of ~1.96, so the coefficient is statistically significant at the 5% level (in fact also significant at the 1% level, where the critical value is ~2.58). The Newey-West adjustment inflates the standard error to account for autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity in the time series of monthly Fama-MacBeth coefficients — necessary here because both MD&A language and supply-chain relationships are highly persistent from one period to the next, so consecutive coefficient estimates are not independent draws. A naive standard error that assumed independence would understate the true uncertainty and could report a t-stat that looks more significant than it actually is.
</details>

**Q5.** <em>Which Voices Move Markets</em> reports an out-of-sample Spearman IC of 0.142 (vs. 0.115 in-sample) and a monthly long-short alpha of 2.03% with t = 6.49. Using the rule of thumb from [[Alpha Factor]] that IC > 0.10 is considered quite good, how should the fact that OOS IC is *higher* than in-sample IC be interpreted?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
An IC of 0.142 clears the "quite good" threshold (0.10) comfortably, and a t-stat of 6.49 on the associated long-short alpha is a strong significance result by any conventional standard (t > 3 already exceeds the higher bar recommended once multiple testing is considered — see [[Overfitting and Multiple Testing]]). More importantly, OOS IC exceeding in-sample IC is the opposite of what you'd expect from an overfit signal — overfitting typically shows up as *in-sample* performance that doesn't survive the OOS split. This pattern (performance holding up, or even improving, OOS with an explicit temporal split) is one of the more reassuring pieces of evidence in this literature, though it is specific to this one paper's speaker-weighted sentiment factor and should not be generalized to every LLM-derived signal without its own OOS test.
</details>

### Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Reality |
|---|---|
| If an LLM-derived factor backtests well on historical text, it's real alpha | It might be — or the LLM's pretraining corpus may have implicitly absorbed retrospective information about the very outcomes being "predicted." Historical-text backtests carry a training-data-contamination risk that price-based factors don't have; live signal generation (as in <em>Signal or Noise</em>) is the strongest available defense, not a routine one. |
| LLM embeddings are just a fancier dictionary-based sentiment score | Embeddings capture context and support graph-based propagation across firms (supply-chain, co-mention networks) — capabilities dictionary word-counting cannot replicate at all, not just a marginal improvement on the same idea. |
| A positive result from a multi-agent LLM system proves the system has genuine skill | It needs to clear the same bars any factor does: significance vs. a random/Monte Carlo baseline, robustness after controlling for known factors, and enough breadth (number of independent bets) to reach statistical significance — <em>Signal or Noise</em>'s own S&P 100 cohort was directionally positive but not significant, precisely because of low breadth. |
| Explainability (e.g., Shapley attribution) is a nice-to-have, not essential, for an LLM alpha factor | In a regulated investment process, an unexplainable black-box signal is difficult to defend to compliance or a regulator; and separately, attributions that don't align with financial reasoning are themselves a warning sign of a spurious correlation rather than genuine information extraction (see <em>Shapley in Context</em>). |
